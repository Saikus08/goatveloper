## Problem

Teams that move from monolithic e-commerce platforms to headless architectures usually gain performance and flexibility, but lose the visual tools that let marketing and content teams operate on their own.

That tradeoff became the core problem of our bachelor’s degree project.

## Approach

We built a SaaS platform that lets non-technical teams edit headless e-commerce storefronts visually, while developers keep control over the underlying architecture and reusable frontend components.

The system was designed around three main pieces:

- a visual editor integrated into the storefront
- a management portal for licenses, onboarding, and team workflows
- an embedded commerce-platform app that acts as the backend bridge

The project was validated with interviews, surveys, and a strong academic delivery process, so it was not only an implementation exercise. It also forced us to justify the product, the architecture, and the business case.

## Why It Matters

What made the project interesting was not only the editor UI. It was the combination of product discovery, technical integration, and operational constraints:

- validating a real market problem with store owners, developers, and commercial profiles
- keeping content native to the commerce platform instead of pushing it into an external CMS
- preserving performance while adding editing capabilities
- making the system production-oriented instead of thesis-only

## What I Learned

This project taught me a lot about how product, architecture, and validation affect each other:

- the value of narrowing scope early to ship a credible first version
- the importance of SSR, state management, and performance discipline in interactive frontend tools
- how much trust preview, rollback, and safe publishing add for non-technical users
- how useful it is to validate technical ideas with both engineering and business stakeholders

## Current Status

The academic version is complete, and the project remains one of the strongest examples of how I think through technical tradeoffs, product constraints, and real user needs.
